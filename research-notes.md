### AgriDigital — worked case study (strong episode material)
In December 2016, AgriDigital executed what it describes as the world's first blockchain settlement of a physical agricultural commodity: grower David Whillock delivered 23.46 metric tons of wheat to Fletcher International Exports in Dubbo, Australia, with title transfer and payment triggered simultaneously by a smart contract on a private Ethereum instance. AgriDigital followed this with a 2017 pilot with CBH Group (Australia's largest grain exporter) at its Blue Lake Milling oats site, and a December 2017 proof-of-concept with Rabobank simulating a three-party structured inventory finance transaction settled with a bank-backed digital dollar. By early 2019 the platform had a network of roughly 1,300 active grain supply-chain users and had processed over 1.6 million metric tons of grain. Source: FAO/ITU "Blockchain for Agriculture" (2019), AgriDigital chapter by Bridie Ohlsson.

### WFP "Building Blocks" — worked case study (strong numbers for a video)
The UN World Food Programme's Building Blocks pilot uses blockchain to manage cash-based food assistance for Syrian refugees in Jordan, integrated with UNHCR's existing iris-scan biometric system so refugees pay for food with an eye scan instead of cash or vouchers — the blockchain layer only changes how the transaction is processed on the back end, not the refugee's experience. WFP reports the pilot cut third-party financial service provider fees by roughly 98%, saving an estimated $150,000/month once scaled to all refugees in Jordanian camps, and reached 100,000 refugees by early 2018 with a goal of covering all 500,000 Jordan-based refugees. Source: FAO/ITU "Blockchain for Agriculture" (2019), Building Blocks chapter by Julia Bacher (WFP Innovation Accelerator).

### AgUnity — worked case study (Kenya + Papua New Guinea)
AgUnity built a low-literacy-friendly smartphone app (large geometric shapes, minimal text) so smallholder farmers in Nanyuki, Kenya and Bougainville, Papua New Guinea could record transactions, plan crop pickups, and trade equipment access on a blockchain-backed ledger, aimed at farmers who'd been burned by opaque or corrupt co-operative record-keeping. Reported results: participating farmers roughly tripled their incomes in one season. In Kenya the constraint was low wheat yields (3-6 bags/acre for smallholders vs 20-26 bags/acre for nearby commercial farms) due to lack of equipment and inability to buy seed at the right planting time; in PNG, roughly half of harvested cacao was spoiling before collection due to poor coordination between farmers and co-operatives — the app's scheduling feature alone recovered most of that loss. Source: FAO/ITU "Blockchain for Agriculture" (2019), AgUnity chapter by Angus Rama Keck.

Founder: Azeez Salawu. This is current, Nigerian, and barely covered — strong first-video or follow-up topic for you.

- **cNGN stablecoin / ReFi DAO Africa piece** — explores blockchain-backed land tokenization and farmer financial inclusion in Nigeria.
- Nigerian government (Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security) has publicly called for AI + blockchain + smart logistics adoption across African agriculture as of mid-2026 — useful for a "policy angle" video.
- **Regulatory friction angle**: Kenyan blockchain lawyer Victoria Kariithi (Mwanyumba Kariithi Consulting) is a citable voice on why unclear regulation is slowing agri-blockchain adoption in Nigeria and Kenya — could be worth reaching out to for an interview-style segment.
- **BitLand / BenBen** — two Ghanaian startups (not agri-specific, but directly relevant) each independently using blockchain to manage land titles and resolve disputes, working with local land-title institutions. Good comparison case for the CAFS Africa / land-documentation angle.
- **FARMS scheme (Kenya)** — a genuinely strong case study for a smallholder-finance episode. Built by ICS (Dutch NGO), Agrics (East African agri-input company serving ~30,000 farmers in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda), EARS (Dutch remote-sensing firm specializing in drought indices), and COIN22 (blockchain mobile-wallet fintech). Instead of pushing full insurance (which smallholders often distrust and can't afford), FARMS lets farmers gradually save into a blockchain-based mobile wallet using a drought-index model; funds unlock automatically when satellite data confirms a bad season, and farmers can graduate into overdraft/loan access as they build a savings history. Frames blockchain as a trust-building *savings* tool rather than a hard insurance sell — a nuanced angle most content in this space misses. Source: FAO/ITU "Blockchain for Agriculture" (2019), FARMS chapter by Violanda de Man.

## 4b. "Do You Even Need Blockchain?" — Risk & Limitations (great credibility-builder)

ITU's chapter in the same FAO/ITU publication (authors: Martin Adolph, Ashish Narayan, Hani Eskandar, Suman Mandahar) is the most rigorous "skeptic's view" in your library and pairs well with the market-sizing disagreement in section 7. Key points worth turning into a video:

- **Not every problem needs a blockchain.** The US Department of Homeland Security publishes a decision flowchart: if you don't need a shared multi-party data store, don't need historical immutability, or there's no real trust/control dispute over who runs the data, a normal database is the right call — not blockchain. This is a genuinely useful visual for an episode that pushes back on blockchain-as-default-solution.
- **Public blockchains are slow and energy-hungry.** Bitcoin-style proof-of-work can require roughly 275 kWh per transaction (Digiconomist's Bitcoin Energy Consumption Index) — worth contrasting with Solana's proof-of-stake/proof-of-history design, which ties into your existing Solana ecosystem authority.
- **Standards are still immature.** No mature international standard governs DLT yet; ISO Technical Committee 307 and several ITU-T focus groups are actively working on this as of the report's writing.
- **Smart contracts aren't a substitute for law.** The 2016 Ethereum hard fork (after an exploit let a user withdraw ~$50 million) is the canonical cautionary tale — worth a segment on why "code is law" breaks down in disputes, especially relevant to African regulatory uncertainty (see section 3).
